Pachycephalosaurus is the most famous head-butter in dinosaur history. The strange part? We mostly have its skull. And scientists still argue about whether it butted anything at all. Sixty-nine to sixty-six million years ago, this three-meter, four-hundred-fifty-kilogram plant eater roamed Montana, South Dakota, Wyoming and Alberta, carrying a dome of solid bone up to twenty-five centimeters thick. Its beak cropped plants and its teeth were tiny and leaf-shaped, so the weapon was clearly not the mouth. Bony knobs line the back of that dome, blunt little spikes decorate the snout, and the eyes face forward for a perfect view of whatever it's about to hit. Basically a crash helmet that grew a personality. You see, the skull is nearly everything we have. The short neck, small arms and stiff tail are borrowed from smaller relatives, like Stegoceras and Homalocephale. Then in two thousand eight, researchers modeled its skull and found the dome could handle high-energy, head-to-head collisions. Case closed? Not quite. That same study says big adults probably relied on showing off and flank-butting instead. So the most famous head-butter may have shoved ribs, or just shown off, like a deer with antlers. Scientists even argue whether its spiky cousins, Stygimoloch and Dracorex, were separate animals or just teenagers. Hell Creek's biggest head-butter, and we're still not sure it ever butted heads. Call it a skull in search of a story. Hard-headed? Definitely.
